The Honest Mockument

Mockument — the app you can walk, with its reasons attached.

Version 1.0 · 18 August 2026 · Status: working method · Part of the Honest framework

The name

MOCK·UP + DOC·UMENT → MOCK·UMENT

A mockument is half mock-up, half document — and so is the word. The front of one, the back of the other, joined into a single name where both halves are still visible. That is not decoration. Showing what you are made of is the first thing this method asks of everything inside it, and the name goes first.

What you are handed is something you can move through like the app itself, carrying the writing that explains what each part means, where it came from, and what nobody has decided yet. It looks like the app, and it tells you which parts are real. That tension is the point: convincing enough to walk through, honest enough to argue with.

Saying it makes the thing concrete. "Send me the mockument for the approvals page" names an object with an owner, a version and a state of readiness — not a stage of a process or a folder of drafts. Teams walk a mockument, question it, mark it up, and build from it.

It is a new word because it names a new thing. Neither half is subordinate: take away the mock-up and it stops being walkable; take away the document and it stops being true.

What this document is

The complete method for building a mockument: turning "we need an app that does this" into something a business person can walk through, a designer can argue with, and an implementer — human or agent — can build from without inventing anything.

It has three parts:

  • The model — what has to be described about a business app, and who is qualified to answer each question.
  • The representation — how that description becomes a navigable, deliberately crude version of the app, so it can be experienced rather than read.
  • The mockument page — the structure that holds the two together, one page of the mockument per page of the app, with every visible thing carrying its own status, its own source, and the name of whoever owes the missing answer.

The single rule everything else follows from: a silence must be visible. Anything nobody has decided is marked as undecided and names the person who must decide it. Everything else in here is machinery for making that rule survive contact with a deadline.

Part 1 · Why the Honest Mockument exists

1. What a text specification loses

A written specification can be correct and still be unusable. It grows to hundreds of pages, and the reader cannot see where any single requirement lands in the app. Requirement 4.7.3 is true, agreed and invisible: nobody can tell which screen it governs, whether two requirements contradict each other on the same screen, or what a person would actually do with the result.

Worse, a text specification captures the path where everything works and quietly loses everything else: the list that gets too long, the figure that is calculated wrongly, the message that fails to send, the person who sees something they should not. None of those are technical details. They are business meaning, and only the people doing the work can supply them.

2. What a conventional mock-up hides

An ordinary mock-up has the opposite failure. It looks like the app, so everyone can react to it — but the reasons are gone. Looking at a screen, a reader cannot tell whether a given element is:

  • a requirement somebody stated;
  • a design proposal;
  • a placeholder; or
  • an invention made while drawing, because the screen looked empty without it.

All four look identical once rendered. The more convincing the mock-up, the more completely the distinction disappears, and the more likely it is that a reviewer nods at something nobody ever asked for.

3. The invention problem

Both failures converge on the same outcome. A question nobody asked still gets an answer during the build — by a developer at 4pm, or by an agent filling a gap in a prompt — and by the time anyone looks, that answer is indistinguishable from a decision. Nobody can say which parts of the product were specified and which were improvised.

This is now the dominant risk rather than a marginal one. An implementing agent does not ignore a specification; it fills every silence in it with something plausible, instantly, and at scale. The defence is not a longer document. It is a document in which silence is visible — where an unanswered question is a marked object with an owner's name on it, and a thing invented to make a screen look finished is labelled as exactly that.

4. What a mockument produces

One artefact, readable four ways:

  • An app you can walk. A navigable, low-fidelity version of the proposed app: menu on the left, pages that open, links that lead somewhere, page conditions you can inspect.
  • A specification you can read. Beside every represented page, the written meaning of everything on it — what it is for, who may see it, what each control does, what happens when things go wrong.
  • A register you can chase. Every open question in the app, by owner, with what it blocks and when it is needed.
  • A contract a machine can hold. A structured record per page that a checker validates and a builder consumes, with stable identifiers that survive into commits, tickets and tests.

It is detailed enough to make the app concrete, and not detailed enough to pretend that unanswered questions have been settled.

Part 2 · The model: what must be described

5. Who answers what

Most specification pain comes from asking the wrong person. A business user cannot say how a system reconnects after a dropped connection, and should never be asked. An implementer cannot decide who is allowed to see a customer's file, and should never be left to.

Question Answered by
What the work is, and its steps Business user
What data the work involves, and what each piece means Business user
Who may see what, and what differs between them Business user
What the app should remember about you between visits Business user
How a list is ordered, filtered, grouped and selected Business user
What can go wrong, as a person would notice it Business user
What should happen when it does Business user
When a figure is as of, and when a person can see it Business user, with the data owner
What the underlying cause of a failure is Implementer
Reconnecting, retrying, resuming Implementer
Storage, sessions, performance, infrastructure Implementer

Two of these are routinely got wrong.

"What can go wrong" means what the person would notice — "the message might not send", "the number on the tile disagrees with the table". It does not mean the cause — "the queue backed up". The noticeable failure is business meaning and belongs in the specification. The cause belongs to whoever builds it.

"What the app remembers about you between visits" is invisible to the person answering. Read and unread, the last filter used, which tab opens first — these feel like facts about the world rather than decisions somebody made. They are decisions, and they will never be volunteered. Ask directly, every page, or they get invented in the build.

6. The objects

A business app represents one or more business processes in a business domain. It records who uses it, including agents as users in their own right.

A business process has a data dictionary, a record of what the app remembers about each user between visits, and one or more process steps.

A process step has a subset of the data dictionary. It records who may see it and what differs between them; what can go wrong and what should happen then, as a person would notice it; whether anything changes while a person is looking at it; and, for each piece of its data that is a list, how the list is ordered, filtered, grouped, selected and presented at zero, one and overflow.

An interactive control does one of three things, and the wording matters because it decides who answers the next question: it acts on data, it alters the screen, or both. A control that acts on data may also need information only the server holds, which is recorded but not designed here.

A data definition has a business meaning, cardinality (one or many), structure (value or record), and is as of some point in time, becomes visible to users some interval after that point, and comes from somewhere — held by us, supplied by a provider, entered by the user, or computed. Part 5 covers how far this is taken, and when.

Any part of any of these may be marked "nobody has answered this yet", naming who must answer it. That marker is not an administrative nicety; it is the mechanism the whole method rests on.

7. Two things about the shape

List behavior belongs together. Ordering, filtering, grouping, selection, nesting, empty behavior and overflow behavior all affect the work. Keeping them in one record makes the list understandable without forcing reviewers to assemble its meaning from separate places.

The unanswered marker is the part most likely to be dropped as bureaucracy, and it is the reason the rest works. A question nobody asked still gets answered during the build, and by then it looks like a decision. Marking it costs one line and keeps the difference visible for as long as the difference matters.

8. What is deliberately outside the model

Reconnecting, retrying and resuming after a failure. Session handling. Storage. Caching. Performance. Infrastructure. Every one of these belongs to whoever builds the app, and none of them improves by being guessed at in a business conversation.

The single exception is whether meaningful business information changes while a page remains open without the current person directly causing it. Somebody watching a queue reorder because new work arrived has a business problem, not merely a technical one. B08 records the expected experience while leaving polling, WebSockets, retries and reconnection to implementation.

When an implementation constraint does change the business experience — a figure that cannot be produced more often than monthly, a permission that cannot be enforced at the row level — the constraint and its consequence come back into the specification as something to discuss, not as a footnote in a technical document nobody reads.

Part 3 · The mockument: how the app is represented

9. What a person sees

A mockument is a set of web pages. On the left is the proposed menu of the real app; selecting an item opens the page the real app would open. Destinations nobody has drawn yet stay in the menu, marked as undrawn, because a gap you can see is worth more than a tidy diagram.

The page itself is not a visual design. It is a high-level representation showing the parts that carry meaning: headings, information, lists, inputs, actions, page conditions and links. Elements that would lead somewhere in the real app lead somewhere here too. A person can click from an item in a queue to the record that closes it, exactly as they would at work, and discover that the two live on different pages and that every walkthrough participant tried to do both in one place.

Beside and beneath the representation is its written specification: what work the page supports, who may see it, what each element shows, what each control means, what the app remembers, what may change while it is open, what a person notices when something fails, and what nobody has answered yet.

The result is simultaneously a document and an app. It can be read in order, explored through the menu, or followed as a piece of work from beginning to end.

10. What a mockument is for

  • Testing whether the menu and page structure match the way people think about their work.
  • Walking real work from beginning to end, including the paths where things go wrong.
  • Connecting every visible element to a business requirement — or exposing that it has none.
  • Revealing requirements that are missing, contradictory or impossible to represent.
  • Comparing what different people see, by switching role rather than by reading a table.
  • Recording observations and unanswered questions where they arise, in context.
  • Giving business users, designers, implementers and agents one shared object to argue about.
  • Producing a starting point for the build that does not require anybody to invent.

It is not for deciding infrastructure, storage, session handling, retry behaviour or any other matter that belongs to the implementer.

11. The objects of representation

A mockument represents one business app and one version of its description. It has a navigation structure, represented pages, workflows, observations, unanswered questions, and a record of the assumptions and proposals that were needed to make it navigable.

The navigation structure is the proposed menu and the other routes through the app: what a person can open, from where, and as which kind of user. Unrepresented destinations are shown and marked.

A represented page is the high-level form of one page of the proposed app. It names the business activity it supports and who may see it. It contains representations of outputs, inputs, controls, messages and links, together with the written explanation of each.

A represented element is anything a person can see, hear, enter, select or activate. It is deliberately low fidelity: it communicates meaning, hierarchy and behaviour without settling typography, colour, spacing or final wording — unless one of those is itself a requirement, in which case it is recorded as one.

A workflow is an application-level graph representing a real piece of work. Its canonical record stores stable IDs for a starting step, page and optional page surface at each step, and transitions tied to actual B04 actions or named business events. Names are displayed to people but never used as the connection. Branches, terminal outcomes and exceptional paths are explicit, so renaming a page does not break the workflow and deleting a referenced surface produces a visible graph error.

An observation records something learned by looking at or walking through the mockument. It is not automatically a decision and never automatically a requirement. It may expose a conflict, an awkward transition, a missing page condition, an unnecessary step, or a question worth asking.

A proposal is a representational choice made so the mockument can be experienced at all. It stays visibly a proposal until the appropriate person accepts it. Acceptance updates the description when the decision concerns business meaning.

An unanswered question states what is unknown, where it matters, who must answer it, and by when. It is visible both in the written material and at the affected place in the mockument.

12. Fidelity, and the five statuses

A mockument may behave like an app, and it must say where the resemblance ends. It uses example data, predetermined outcomes and simulated state changes. It does not prove that an integration works, that rules are complete, that permissions are enforced, that calculations are correct, that anything persists, or that performance is acceptable.

Every meaningful part carries exactly one status:

Status Meaning
required An answered requirement supports it.
proposed Introduced to make the representation concrete. Nobody has accepted it.
unanswered A decision is still needed. The marker names who must make it.
observed Learned during review. Not yet a decision, and not a requirement.
out of scope Deliberately not represented here.

Two disciplines make the statuses more than decoration.

The overlay. One control tints the whole mock by status, so a reviewer sees at a glance how much of the screen in front of them is actually agreed. The first run of this on a finished-looking page is usually uncomfortable. That discomfort is the mockument working.

No unmarked elements. An element drawn without a status fails the readiness check. Silence about provenance is treated as a defect, not as a default.

Visual polish must never disguise uncertainty. A convincing screen is not evidence that its contents or its behaviour have been agreed.

13. What a mockument does not settle

  • Final visual design, typography, colour or wording — except where wording is itself a requirement.
  • Backend architecture and data model.
  • Whether an integration is technically possible.
  • Production permission enforcement and security.
  • Reconnection, retrying, resuming, sessions, storage.
  • Performance, accessibility compliance, browser support.
  • Cost and schedule.

These are investigated elsewhere. Where one of them constrains the business experience, the constraint returns here as something to discuss.

Part 4 · The mockument page

This part is the addition the Honest Mockument makes to both practices: a fixed anatomy that carries the model and the representation together, page by page, in a form that is equally legible to a business reviewer and to a machine.

14. One page per page

One page of the app is one page of the mockument. Not one long document with sections, and not one screen per section of a scrolling page.

The reason is not tidiness. A specification that scrolls cannot be walked: every reader arrives at the top and travels downward, which is nothing like using an app. When each page of the app is its own page of the mockument, the mockument's own navigation is the app's proposed menu, and reviewing the structure of the app becomes the same act as reading the document. Questions like "can I get back from here?" and "why is this three clicks away?" answer themselves.

Within one Mockument page, B01–B13 are focused block views rather than thirteen more application pages. The status dashboard is their menu: it remains pinned in one viewport position while selecting a block replaces the working panel without scrolling. B01 includes the page overview, identity, metadata, page tools and overall readiness; B02–B13 show only their selected block. The application page, browser title, autosave record and export remain the same. Previous and Next support sequential work; a block hash supports deep links. An explicit all-block view exists only for printing, browser search and full-document review.

The consequences are practical:

  • Every page has one owner, one version and one readiness state.
  • Two people can work on two pages without conflict.
  • A page can be sent to somebody on its own and still make sense.
  • The register pages — questions, fields, routes — can be assembled mechanically from the set.

15. The thirteen blocks

Every page carries the same thirteen blocks in the same order. Blocks B01–B02 are identity, B03 defines the available business data, B04 is the mock and its records, B05–B10 complete the specification, and B11–B13 are the honesty apparatus. Each block names who answers it.

Identity

B01 · Page header. Name, business activity, route, build status, version, date and owner, followed by the readiness stages. Business activity explains what work the page supports, why someone opens it, and what they should leave with. Answered by the page owner.

B02 · Roles. Every listed role can access the page. Record what differs between roles and what each must not see; omit roles that cannot access the page. The B02 block assignee owns the role register, so individual role rows do not repeat answer ownership. Business user.

Data and the mock

B03 · Data definitions. Stable data ID, business name, meaning, cardinality (one or many), structure (value or record), unit, as-of point, availability, provenance, calculation, whether a person can change it, example value and data-ladder rung. Definitions can be provisional while the mock reveals missing data. Business user for meaning and timing; data owner for source and calculation.

B04 · Mock composer. The navigable representation and canonical records for sections, static content, data-bound components, notices and actions. A data-bound component selects a B03 definition before it can choose a compatible presentation. Many-item data may be a list or table; lists define one item and tables define named columns. Presentation and data shape are related but never treated as the same concept.

The standing fidelity qualification is permanent system text on every mock and an explicit rule in the build contract; it is not a reviewed page block.

The remaining specification

B05 · Page conditions. The default condition is automatic. Record only a named departure from default that materially changes the page’s message, major visibility, available business actions, ability to continue, or required next step. Sorting, filtering, selected rows, moved columns and expanded sections remain B04 component behavior. Each condition records when it occurs, what changes, affected component IDs, unavailable actions and what happens next. Business user.

B06 · What can go wrong. Described as the person notices it, never as a cause; what should happen; who is told. Business user.

B07 · Remembered between visits. What the app remembers about this person, for how long, and whether they can clear it. Asked directly because it is never volunteered. Business user.

B08 · Updates while the page is open. First answer whether nothing changes unless the person acts or refreshes, or whether information may change while open. Only the latter requires update records. Each record names the business event, affected B03 data and B04 components, expected timing and update method, how the person is told, collection movement, protection for in-progress or stale work, and any resulting B05 condition. Sorting, filtering and moved columns remain B04 behavior because the current person causes them directly. A calculation belongs to B03; its trigger and immediate progress belong to B04; an asynchronous result arriving later belongs here. Business user.

B09 · Connections. Where a person arrives from, where they can go, and which shared components this page uses. Rendered as links, so the graph is walkable in both directions.

B10 · Workflow participation. A generated local view of every canonical application workflow that references this page. It shows the stable step, targeted page root or B04 surface, incoming transitions, outgoing action or business-event transitions, branch conditions and terminal outcomes. Canonical graphs are managed in Settings; B10 never asks somebody to retype page, tab, wizard-step or section names.

The honesty apparatus

B11 · Copy register. Every user-visible string, each marked required wording or placeholder. Wording is where an unattended builder invents most freely; a placeholder that is labelled is safe, an unlabelled one becomes product.

B12 · Decisions, observations, questions and scope. One compact register whose first field declares the record’s authority. A decision is accepted and names its scope, owner, date, rationale, lifecycle, superseded decision and the canonical records it changed. An observation preserves what a walkthrough taught without granting it authority. A question remains unresolved and names why it matters, what it blocks, who owes the answer and when. A scope record deliberately excludes something and explains why. All types can reference affected stable IDs; selecting the type reveals only its relevant fields.

B13 · Build contract and machine-readable record. The instruction block handed to whoever builds this page — what to build, what not to invent, what to ask about first, what done means — and the structured record that the checker validates and the builder consumes.

16. The mock frame

The frame is what turns a picture into an instrument. Three controls sit above every mock.

Role. Switching role hides and reveals elements according to the permissions block, so "what does a supervisor see?" is answered by looking rather than by reading. What a role must not see is stated beside it.

Page condition. Default is always available. Any additional option is a B05 page-level condition, not a sort order, filter, column arrangement or other component setting. Selecting a condition annotates the mock with what changes from default, which components are affected, which actions become unavailable, and what the person must do next.

Honesty overlay. Tints every element by its status. Amber is proposed, pink is unanswered, green is required, grey is out of scope. An element with no status shows as unanswered, because that is what it is.

The inspector. Clicking any element opens its full record: status, what it shows, where it comes from, where it leads, what it traces to, and every open question attached to it. Clicking an element that has no record is the most useful outcome of all — it means somebody drew a thing nobody asked for, and the frame says so plainly.

Links inside the mock frame navigate. That is the difference between an app that can be walked and a picture of one.

17. Identity, IDs and traceability

Every element, control, question and requirement carries a stable identifier: EL-07, CTL-03, Q-02, REQ-MON-11. This is not bureaucracy; it is what makes the specification addressable.

  • In a review: "EL-07 is wrong" rather than "the table thing on the second tab".
  • In a ticket: the ID travels with the work.
  • In the build: the same ID becomes the test identifier, so a page can be checked against its spec mechanically.
  • In the register: one query answers "where does this requirement appear?" and another answers "what justifies this element?"

Traceability runs both ways. From a requirement you can find every place it appears in the mockument. From an element you can find the requirement, proposal, observation or unanswered question that accounts for it. When an element traces to nothing, that is not a gap in the paperwork — it is an element nobody asked for, and it should either acquire a requirement or leave the page.

Part 5 · The data layer

18. Which data questions can change a page

Field-level work is the most expensive part of any specification and the easiest to do too early. The way out is to notice that data questions divide cleanly into two kinds.

Questions that can invalidate a page. Does this number exist at all? Who supplies it? What point in time is it as of, and how long after that can a person see it? Is it a stored fact or something we compute? — If a figure is only produced at month end and only lands several business days later, then a screen offering "today's number" is dead, and no amount of layout review will reveal that. These are design questions wearing a data costume. They cost about thirty seconds each, they are answered by the business, and they belong in the mockument from the first day.

Questions that can only change the code. Which provider field, which database column, the exact formula, the display format and decimals, the routing when the same figure comes from two providers, the reconciled value for a known example. None of these can invalidate a screen. They are also the bulk of the work — in a typical dictionary, most fields are calculated rather than copied — so doing them for a page that later gets cut is pure waste.

The Honest Mockument therefore does not treat field detail as a phase. It treats it as a ladder, climbed per page, so nothing above the bottom rung is ever paid for on a page that does not survive review.

19. The four rungs

Rung What is answered When Recorded as

Three rules keep the ladder honest.

Promotion is per page, not per project. A field climbs when a page that uses it needs it to. The same field may sit at L2 because one page is being built and be irrelevant everywhere else.

A field defined once is referenced everywhere. Fields carry a shared identifier, so a figure that appears on nine screens has one definition, one source and one calculation. Where two pages describe the same field differently, the register shows the disagreement instead of averaging it away.

Hero fields go to L2 immediately. Some numbers are the product: a score, a ranking, a a figure somebody would have to defend under scrutiny. Validating pages that display a wrong number proves nothing, so a field marked as hero must reach L2 while the mockument is still being drawn. Expect five to ten of these in an app, not fifty.

The stale rule is not the null rule. "Empty" and "older than it should be" are different business situations with different answers, and the second one is the one that actually happens in production.

20. Controlled vocabularies

Three fields use fixed vocabularies. Free text in these columns cannot be counted, filtered, tested or trusted, and drifts into dozens of near-synonyms within a few months.

Periodicity — what point in time the value is as of:

  • live
  • end of previous business day
  • end of previous month
  • latest closed period
  • on demand
  • reference / on change

A project may extend this list with a period its own work actually uses — a trading day, a filing, a shift, a billing cycle — but it extends the list deliberately, in one place, and does not free-text the column.

Two things that are not periodicities. A derived rate is not one: a figure expressed per year is a calculation, computed as of a point that is itself one of the values above. And a refresh cadence is not one either: a weekly job that reloads figures published quarterly does not make the data weekly.

Visibility — how long after that point a person can see it: stated as a business interval ("next business day", "second to fourth business day of the month", "immediately on save"), never as a job name.

Provenance — who supplies it: held by us · a provider · the user · computed · nobody knows.

Nuance that does not fit the vocabulary goes in an adjacent note, never in the vocabulary field itself.

Part 6 · Readiness

21. The three gates

Readiness is computed from the specification, not asserted in a meeting. Each page reports three gates and, when one fails, exactly what is in the way.

Gate 1 — ready to walk through

  • Every page names the step it represents.
  • Every element and control carries a status.
  • Every open question names who must answer it.
  • Every page has a purpose written in business words.
  • The menu and the workflows navigate.

Meaning: somebody can be shown this without a guide.

Gate 2 — ready for a business review

Everything in gate 1, plus:

  • Every role listed can reach the page, and what differs between roles is recorded.
  • Every applicable page-level condition says when it occurs, what changes from default, which components or actions are affected, and what the person does next. Pages with no material departure from default do not invent conditions.
  • What can go wrong is recorded as a person would notice it.
  • Every field is at least at L0, and every hero field is at L2.

Meaning: the ordinary path, the important alternatives, the role differences and the user-visible failures can all be inspected. A business person can approve or reject this and be right either way.

Gate 3 — ready to inform implementation

Everything in gates 1 and 2, plus:

  • No question that blocks the build remains open.
  • No element remains unanswered.
  • Every element traces to a requirement, or is explicitly marked as having none.
  • What the app remembers between visits, and what changes while it is looked at, are both settled.
  • No user-visible string is still a placeholder.
  • Every field on the page is at least at L2.
  • Accepted proposals have been written back into the description.

Meaning: an implementer — human or agent — can start without inventing anything, and without asking the same question twice.

22. What the gates are not

Gate 3 is not "the implementation is designed". Architecture, storage and integration remain untouched by it. It says only that the business questions are closed.

Gates are not permission to skip review. A page can pass every mechanical check and still be the wrong page. The gates detect silence, not error.

Gates must never be satisfied by editing the data. Marking an unanswered element as required to turn a light green is the one act that destroys the mockument. If a gate is inconvenient, the honest moves are to answer the question or to accept that the page is not ready.

A failing gate is not a failure. Early on, every page fails gate 3 and should. The useful reading is the trend and the reason list, not the colour.

24. Running a walkthrough

A walkthrough is a working session, not a presentation. One person drives the mock; the business participants do the talking; somebody records observations and questions in the page as they arise rather than in a separate notebook that is never reconciled.

The agenda — nine questions a mockument exists to provoke:

  1. Does the menu describe the way people actually think about their work?
  2. Can each person reach what they need without seeing what they should not?
  3. Does one step require several pages here — and if so, is the step too big?
  4. Can a person tell where they are, what just happened, and what they can do next?
  5. What happens when a list is empty, holds exactly one item, or holds more than fits?
  6. What happens when a person cannot complete what they came to do?
  7. Does each workflow have a beginning, a useful ending, and understandable exceptional paths?
  8. Is every visible element backed by a requirement, or was it invented to complete the picture?
  9. What business decision has this session exposed that nobody had made?

The questions to ask a business user, in the order that works. Ask them as written; the wording is doing work.

  • What is the work, and what are its steps?
  • What data does this work involve, and what does each piece mean?
  • Who may see this, and what differs between them? What must somebody not see?
  • What should the app remember about you between visits?
  • What order does this list appear in? How may it be filtered or grouped? Can items be picked out, and what happens then? Does anything sit inside anything else, and how deep?
  • What appears when this list holds nothing? When it holds only one? When it holds more than fits here?
  • What can go wrong, as you would notice it — and what should happen when it does?
  • Can meaningful information change while the page remains open without this person causing it? If so, when should it appear, how are they told, and what protects in-progress work?
  • What is this number as of, and how long after that can somebody see it?

During the session: switch role and each applicable page condition on the mock frame rather than describing them; turn the honesty overlay on at least once; and when somebody says "obviously it would…", stop and record it as a question, because that sentence is the single most reliable indicator of an unexamined assumption.

After the session: every observation is filed as an observation, not promoted to a requirement; every question gets an owner and a date before the meeting ends; and any decision taken in the room is recorded with the name of the person who took it.

25. Filling a page with an agent

The structured record makes it safe to have an agent draft a mockument page, provided the instruction makes silence illegal. The rules, in order of importance:

  1. Never invent an answer to make the page look finished. If nobody has stated it, mark it unanswered and add a question naming who must answer it and by when.
  2. Anything drawn to make the mock concrete is proposed, with a note saying why it was drawn.
  3. Describe what can go wrong as the person would notice it, never as a technical cause.
  4. Ask, explicitly, the two things nobody volunteers: what the app remembers between visits, and whether meaningful information may change while the page remains open without the current person causing it.
  5. For every list: order, filtering, selection, nesting, empty, one and more-than-fits behavior.
  6. Every element in the mock needs a row with a stable ID; every row needs either a trace to a requirement or a status admitting it has none.
  7. A string is required wording only if somebody confirmed the exact words. Otherwise it is a placeholder.
  8. Fields answer L0 only, unless the field is a hero field, in which case take it to L2.
  9. When finished, run the checker, report which gates pass, and list what would have to be answered — and by whom — to pass the next one. Never adjust the data to make a gate pass.

26. Building from a page

Each page ends with a build contract, addressed to whoever implements it:

  • Build exactly this — the components, actions, page conditions and wording that are required.
  • Do not invent — the specific things that are drawn but unspecified, named individually.
  • Stop and ask first — the open questions that touch this page, with their owners.
  • Done means — the acceptance checks, including that every element identifier survives into the built page as a test identifier.

Most of that contract can be handed over mechanically rather than retyped: see the Annex.

That last point is what keeps the specification alive after the build starts. When EL-07 exists in the spec, in the ticket and in the test, a change to any of the three is visible in the others. When it exists only in prose, the specification becomes historical within a fortnight.

27. Change, versioning and write-back

Every page carries a version, a date and an owner. A review is repeatable only if a reader can see what moved since last time.

Accepted proposals are written back. When somebody accepts a proposal that concerns business meaning, the status changes from proposed to required and the underlying description is updated. A proposal that is accepted in a meeting but left marked proposed is worse than one nobody discussed, because it now looks examined.

Observations are not silently promoted. An observation becomes a requirement only by somebody deciding it, on a date, under their name.

Questions close by being answered, not by expiring. A question whose date passes is escalated, not deleted. If it is genuinely no longer relevant, it is closed with a note saying why.

The description leads the representation. Where the two disagree, the description wins and the mockument is corrected — never the reverse, and never silently.

28. The minimal page record

If nothing else is captured for a page, capture these nine things. Everything in §15 is an expansion of this list.

  1. Name and route — how the page is reached.
  2. Business activity — what work the page supports, why someone opens it, and what they should leave with.
  3. Roles — the roles that can access it, and what differs between them.

  5. Page conditions — material departures from default that a reviewer can inspect.
  6. Links and actions — where they lead, and what business effect they represent.
  7. Traceability — the requirement behind each meaningful element.
  8. Proposals and assumptions — what was added to make the representation concrete.
  9. Observations and unanswered questions — what the walkthrough revealed, and who must respond.

31. Glossary

As of — the point in time a value describes, as distinct from when it was fetched or displayed.

Build contract — the instruction block on each page telling an implementer what to build, what not to invent, what to ask about first, and what done means.

Gate — a computed readiness check. Three of them: walk-through, business review, implementation.

Hero field — a number that is itself the product, and must be defined to L2 while the mockument is still being drawn.

Honesty overlay — a control that tints the mock frame by status, showing at a glance how much of a screen is actually agreed.

Mock frame — the navigable representation of one page inside a mockument page, with its role, page-condition and overlay controls.

Mockument — half mock-up, half document: a navigable representation of an app in which every visible thing carries what backs it, who owes the missing answer, and whether it is ready to build.

Observation — something learned by walking through. Not a decision and not a requirement.

Page condition — a named, material departure from the automatic default that changes the page’s message, major visibility, available business actions, ability to continue, or required next step. Component settings such as sorting and moved columns are not page conditions.

Periodicity — which of the agreed fixed answers describes what a value is as of.

Proposal — a representational choice made so the mockument can be experienced, awaiting acceptance.

Provenance — who supplies a field: held by us, a provider, the user, computed, or nobody knows.

Represented element — anything in a mockument a person can see, hear, enter, select or activate.

Rung — how far a field has been taken: L0 exists, L1 display, L2 sourced, L3 verified.

Stale rule — what a screen shows when a value is older than it should be. Distinct from the null rule.

Status — one of: required, proposed, unanswered, observed, out of scope.

Step — a unit of work that stays the same across devices; the thing that holds business meaning.

Translation bundle — the set of files generated from a mockument page in the shapes the target framework expects. A translator, never a designer. See the Annex.

Unanswered — a decision that has not been made, recorded with the name of whoever must make it.

Annex · The translation bundle

32. What the bundle is

The method stops at the third gate: the business questions are closed, and an implementer can start. The bundle is what makes that last step mechanical rather than a retyping exercise. One command reads a mockument page and writes out the same content in the shapes the target framework expects — components, page surfaces, wiring, display formats, test scenarios and fixtures.

It is a translator, not a designer. It adds nothing. Every line it writes is either already in the page's structured record, or an explicit note that the record does not answer it, naming the person who must. A silence stays a silence, in the builder's own file format.

That distinction is the whole reason it can be trusted to run unattended. A generator that fills gaps with plausible defaults would undo the method in a single command.

33. What it emits

Per page, into a handoff/<page>/ folder:

File What it holds
components/…/manifest.json One folder per element: its name, tier, data-component value and parameters, in the framework's component format.
components/…/<block>.html · <block>.css The template and the namespaced stylesheet, carrying the element's ID and status as comments so the origin travels with the code.
components/…/style.json For organisms: the CSS custom properties the component references, declared with descriptions and no values — values belong to the theme, not to the component.
components/…/provenance.json Which element the component came from, its status, what it traces to, its open questions, and which parts of the manifest were inferred rather than specified.
page/page.py · page/page.html The page scaffold: declared surfaces including the notification zone, the role list, the page-condition list, and the includes and wiring the page emits.
page/routes.py A stub for every control that needs data only the server knows.
wiring.md Every control, what it becomes, what it targets, and whether its destination exists.
format.md Display attributes derived from the data ladder's L1 rung, and the fields still below it.
features/<page>.feature Scenarios generated from applicable page conditions × roles, plus the automatic default and one per user-noticed failure.
fixtures.json The sample values the mock draws, so the built page can boot showing the same data.
contract.md The build contract from the page, verbatim.
unresolved.md Everything a builder may not invent. The first file to read.

Two of these earn their place immediately. wiring.md predicts, before any code exists, which controls point at destinations that do not: a control whose target page has not been drawn is a wire that reaches nowhere, and the framework's own gate will refuse it. unresolved.md is the page's silences collected in one place — blocking questions with owners and dates, elements that are proposed or unanswered, fields below the sourced rung, and copy still marked placeholder.

34. How a mockument maps onto the framework

The mockument answers The framework consumes it as
Elements, and the shared component register Components — atoms, molecules and organisms, each owning its own prefix and data-component name
A control's business effect Page wiring. Acts on data → a route on an organism. Alters the screen → client state functions, no route. Both → both
Whether a control needs data only the server knows The tier itself: it cannot be an atom or a molecule
The L1 rung — unit, null rule, stale rule Display attributes on the element that holds the value, which is the single place its display is decided
The L2 rung — source, calculation, dependencies The typed boundary where raw request values are classified, and the persistence behind it
Applicable page conditions × roles, the automatic default, and the failures table Generated test scenarios
"Who is told" in the failures table Messages routed to the page's notification surface
Who may see it The authorisation layer
The copy register Template strings, with placeholder as a lint rather than a shipped decision

The mapping is what changes between ecosystems; the content being mapped does not. A mockument written once can be emitted for whichever stack the project actually uses.

35. What the emitter infers, and marks

Three things cannot be read directly from the page, so the emitter proposes them and records that it did — each one appears in unresolved.md and in each component's provenance.json:

  1. Component tier. Inferred from whether an element carries a collection and whether it needs a source. Confirm before building.
  2. Route paths and ownership. Paths are proposed from the control's name. Which organism owns which route is genuinely not answered by the mockument, and is left open rather than assigned.
  3. Style token names. A small standard set per component. The values are absent by design.

Nothing else is inferred. If a fourth guess ever becomes necessary, it belongs in this list before it belongs in the output.

36. What it refuses to do

It will not choose a colour or a density. It will not invent a display format for a field that has not reached the display rung. It will not name a currency or a date format nobody has chosen. It will not decide whether meaningful information updates while a page remains open without the current person causing it. It will not turn a placeholder string into product copy.

Each of those refusals appears in the output as a marked gap with an owner, which is the same behaviour the method asks of a person drawing a page.

37. Running it

One command writes the whole bundle; a flag limits it to a single page. Regenerate it; never edit it. The bundle is a projection of the mockument, in the same way the readable specification is a projection of the structured record — editing the projection puts the two out of step and quietly recreates the problem the method exists to remove.

Where a project has adopted a design language, the only human input the bundle needs before code is written is the set of values for the custom properties each component declares. That is a values file, not a design phase, and it is written once per product rather than once per page.

38. Two gates in series

The result is two checks that ask different questions of the same work.

The mockument gate asks did anybody decide this — is every element backed, does every question have an owner, has the data reached the rung this stage requires. It is about silence.

The framework gate asks does this resolve — does every reference the page emits point at something that exists, does every chain type-check, does every dispatch resolve. It is about structure.

Neither can answer the other's question, and a project needs both. A screen can be perfectly wired and built entirely out of things nobody asked for; a page can be fully agreed and wired to a route that was never mounted. Run them in that order — decide, then resolve — and there is no gap between the two where an invention can hide.

A mockument is finished when the difference between what was required, what was proposed, what was observed and what nobody has answered survives all the way from the first interview to the built product. Nothing else in this document matters more than that.
